# Introductions (reference)

> Article Craft editorial craft guidance (INDUSTRY_BEST_PRACTICE). Not a
> Medium rule — but see `../platforms/medium/distribution.md`: the title,
> subtitle, and opening are what readers judge you on in the feed.

## What the first 100 words must do

1. **Earn the click-through to paragraph two** — give the reader a reason
   that survives the title.
2. **Establish the promise** — what will the reader have or know by the end?
3. **Establish credibility** — why are *you* the person telling this?
   (Experience beats credentials.)
4. **Set the scope** — what this article does and does not cover.

## Patterns that work for technical articles

- **The problem in the wild**: a concrete moment where the problem bit you.
  ("Last Tuesday our p99 latency tripled. The cause surprised me.")
- **The surprising claim**: state something the reader believes is false,
  then support it. Requires you to actually support it.
- **The before/after**: what the reader's situation looks like before and
  after applying the article.
- **The misconception**: "Most developers think X. Actually Y." Requires Y
  to be true.
- **The straight promise**: "By the end of this article you will be able to
  X." Honest and effective; the article must deliver.

## Anti-patterns (flagged by the review engine)

- **The throat-clear**: "In today's fast-paced world of software
  development..." — says nothing, delays everything.
- **The dictionary opening**: "X is defined as..." — Wikipedia is right
  there.
- **The resume**: three paragraphs about your background before the reader
  knows why they're here.
- **The buried lede**: the interesting fact appears in paragraph six.
- **The bait-and-switch**: intro promises A, article delivers B (a
  Medium distribution disqualifier when title/subtitle misrepresent).
- **The AI cadence**: "In the ever-evolving landscape of...", "Let's dive
  in!", "It's important to note that..." — generic patterns readers have
  learned to skip.

## Test

Read only the title, subtitle, and first paragraph. Would a smart, busy
reader in your target audience keep going? If you hesitated, the intro
isn't done.
